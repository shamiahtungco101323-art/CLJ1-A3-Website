01 / FIRST PRELIM

Crime, Law
and Justice.

Start with the basic ideas that explain why acts become crimes, how law operates, who may be affected by crime, and how justice is understood.

01

What comes first: the Law or the Crime?

The handout explains that law is something laid down, established, or decreed. It defines crime, treats of its nature, and provides for punishment. If there is no law punishing an act, the act is not treated as a crime under that law. In this sense, crime is presented as a product of law.

02

What is Crime?

Crime is an act or omission punishable by public laws. The handout identifies four specific terms:

Felonies

Violations of the Revised Penal Code.

Offenses under Special Laws

Violations of statutes of special laws.

Infraction / Misdemeanor

Violations of ordinances.

Delinquency

Violations committed by minor offenders.

03

Concepts of Crime, Law and Justice

The 1987 Philippine Constitution provides for the distribution of the great powers of government among three branches: the Executive, Legislative, and Judiciary. These branches are co-equal and observe the separation of powers.

Limitations on legislative power in enacting penal legislation

  • No ex post facto law or bill of attainder shall be enacted.
  • Ex post facto law: makes an act done before the passage of the law punishable.
  • Bill of attainder: a legislative act which inflicts punishment without trial.
  • No person shall be held to answer for a criminal offense without due process of law.
04

Criminal and Victim

A person who commits a crime is basically a criminal. In the criminal justice system, however, different terms may be used at different stages of the justice process depending on the person's status in the case.

SuspectA person suspected of having committed the crime.
RespondentThe person involved at the prosecution level.
AccusedThe person formally charged in court.
ConvictA person found guilty beyond reasonable doubt.
PrisonerA person serving a sentence after conviction.
05

Types of Victim

Primary Victim

The person who directly experiences the harm of the crime. Example: a rape victim.

Secondary Victim

Individuals related to or connected with the primary victim, such as parents, cousins, or siblings.

Tertiary Victim

Other individuals or persons who may be affected by what happened to the victim or by the consequences of the crime.

06

Victimless Crimes

These are crimes against the law where the individual who participates may have consented to the activity. The handout notes that there is no private offended party in the usual sense.

GamblingProstitutionPossession of FirearmsDrug-related offenses
07

Five Pillars of Criminal Justice

Law Enforcement

Deals with the enforcement and initial response to violations of law.

Prosecution

Evaluates complaints and prosecutes criminal actions.

Courts

Determine the legal issues and the guilt or innocence of the accused.

Corrections

Concerned with persons who are under correctional custody or serving sentences.

Community

The community is part of the wider criminal justice process and is affected by crime and justice.

08

Concepts of Justice

Procedural Justice

Fairness in the process, regardless of whether a person is rich or poor.

Distributive Justice

Concerned with the fair distribution of law, benefits, burdens, and penalties.

Restorative Justice

Focuses on restoring or repairing the harm caused by an offense.

09

Evolution of Law

Code of Hammurabi

One of the first known bodies of law. It was promulgated by King Hammurabi of ancient Babylon and engraved on stone tablets. It dealt with issues including theft, ownership, sexual relationships, and violence.

Roman Law

Derived from the Twelve Tables, a collection of basic rules related to family, religion, and economic life.

Common Law

Its origin is traced to early English society. It developed from social customs, rules, and practices.

Magna Carta

Signed on June 15, 1215 by King John of England. The handout describes it as guaranteeing basic liberties for British citizens.

Greek Laws

Contributed to ideas about law through the thoughts of philosophers such as Socrates, Plato, and Aristotle.

Canon Law

Also known as Ecclesiastical Law and adopted for the government of Christian organizations.

Germanic Law

A family of customary laws from Germanic tribes.

Philippine Laws

For early Filipinos, laws were written and unwritten. Written laws were decreed and codified, while unwritten laws were based on customs and traditions. The handout also discusses the Code of Kalantiaw as an alleged pre-Spanish code associated with Datu Sumakwel of Panay.

10

Search Warrant and Warrantless Search

The handout defines a search warrant as an order in writing issued in the name of the People of the Philippines, signed by a judge and directed to a peace officer, commanding the officer to search for personal property described in the warrant and bring it before the court. It has a validity period of ten (10) days and can be served only once within its validity period.

Personal property that may be seized

  • Subject of the offense.
  • Stolen or embezzled property and other proceeds or fruits of the offense.
  • Property used or intended to be used as the means of committing an offense.

Circumstances of warrantless search listed in the handout

  1. Warrantless search incidental to a lawful arrest.
  2. Seizure of evidence in plain view.
  3. Search of a moving vehicle.
  4. Consented warrantless search.
  5. Customs search.
  6. Stop and frisk search.
  7. Exigent and emergency circumstances.
11

Instigation vs. Entrapment

Instigation

The police officer induces a person to commit a crime that otherwise would not be committed. The criminal idea comes from the arresting officer. The handout states that this is not valid and gives a sell-bust operation as an example.

Entrapment

The arresting officer devises ways and means to catch or apprehend a person who has already committed or is already disposed to commit the crime. The criminal mind originates from the accused. The handout gives a buy-bust operation as an example.

12

Other Law Enforcement Agencies

  • National Bureau of Investigation (NBI) — a highly trained investigative body, national in scope, under the Department of Justice.
  • Philippine Coast Guard (PCG) — an armed and uniformed service attached to the Department of Transportation; the handout describes its role concerning unsafe foreign vessels.
  • Philippine Center on Transnational Crime (PCTC) — formulates and implements a concerted program for the prevention and control of transnational crime and is under the direct supervision of the Office of the President.
  • Department of Environment and Natural Resources (DENR) — tasked with enforcement of environmental laws.
  • Bureau of Immigration (BI) — tasked with enforcement of immigration laws.
  • Philippine Drug Enforcement Agency (PDEA) — primarily concerned with enforcement of drug laws and under the Office of the President.

02 / SECOND PRELIM

The Prosecution
Pillar.

The prosecution is the second pillar of the criminal justice system. This section follows the handout's discussion of prosecution and preliminary investigation.

01

What is Prosecution?

Prosecution is the process wherein an accusation is brought before a court of justice to determine the guilt or innocence of the accused. It ensures that persons are tried in a court of law and sentenced if convicted.

The prosecution pillar is primarily concerned with the investigation of the complaint filed before its office.

02

Three Kinds of Prosecution

Accusatorial System

An adversarial system involving two contending parties, the accuser and the accused. The victim or representative has the obligation of finding and presenting evidence to the court.

Inquisitorial System

Investigation and prosecution are left in the hands of the government's prosecutorial arms.

Mixed System

A combination of the accusatorial and inquisitorial systems. The handout identifies this as the system adopted in the Philippine setting.

03

What does “Prosecute” mean?

To prosecute means to officially accuse someone of committing an offense in a court of law, or, for a lawyer, to try to prove that the person accused of committing a crime is guilty of that crime.

04

Who are Prosecutors?

Prosecutors are charged with addressing violations of criminal law and promoting public safety. In carrying out these responsibilities, they must also bear in mind their role as ministers of justice and consider the rights, needs, and interests of members of the community, including victims and individuals charged with criminal conduct.

05

Who must prosecute criminal action?

All criminal cases commenced by complaint or information shall be prosecuted under the direction and control of the public prosecutor. Private prosecutors may be allowed to prosecute when they are under the direction and supervision of the public prosecutor.

06

Role and Functions of Prosecutors

The purpose of a criminal action is to determine the penal responsibility of the accused. The handout explains that, because the People of the Philippines are the offended party in a criminal case, the interest of the private complainant is generally limited to civil liability.

  • Evaluate police findings referred to them and complaints filed directly with their office.
  • File the corresponding information or criminal complaints in the proper courts based on their evaluation of the evidence.
  • Prosecute offenders in court in the name of the People of the Philippines.
07

Relationship of the Prosecutor with Investigating Agencies and Courts

The prosecutor's role at the initial stage is to establish that a crime has probably been committed and that there is a basis to proceed. This requires close coordination with investigating agencies such as the Philippine National Police, National Bureau of Investigation, and quasi-judicial bodies empowered to conduct investigations.

The handout emphasizes coordination between prosecutors and police so that evidence collected can stand up to judicial scrutiny. Courts rely heavily on evidence submitted by the prosecution panel, while the panel must ensure that required evidence is available, properly stored, and that there are no undue delays that could affect the hearing schedule.

08

Preliminary Investigation

Preliminary investigation is the stage of inquiry or proceeding used to determine whether there is sufficient ground to engender a well-founded belief that a crime has been committed and that the respondent is probably guilty and should be held for trial.

Purposes

  1. Determine whether a crime has been committed and whether there is sufficient ground to believe that the respondent is probably guilty and should be held for trial.
  2. Protect the accused from the inconvenience, expense, and burden of defending himself or herself in a formal trial when reasonable probability of guilt has not first been ascertained.
  3. Secure the innocent against hasty, malicious, and oppressive prosecution and an open and public accusation.
  4. Protect the State from useless and expensive trials when the evidence does not support prosecution.
09

Coverage of Preliminary Investigation

The handout discusses the 2024 Department of Justice–National Prosecution Service Rules on Preliminary Investigations and Inquest Proceedings. It states that preliminary investigation proceedings shall be required for crimes or offenses where the penalty prescribed by law is six (6) years and one (1) day without regard to fine, subject to the rules and exceptions described in the handout.

The handout also notes that the prosecutor may dismiss a complaint, with the required approval, when there is no prima facie evidence with reasonable certainty of conviction.

10
