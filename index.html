FIRST PRELIM — Historical Development and Principles of Criminal Justice

1. Concept of Crime and Justice

What comes first, the law or the crime?

  • Law is that which is laid down, established or decreed.
  • Most laws are penal in nature, meaning they define crime, treat of their nature, and provide for their punishment.
  • Nullum crimen nulla poena sine lege — there is no crime where there is no law punishing it.
  • Thus, crime is a product of the law.
“Nullum crimen nulla poena sine lege” means that an act cannot be treated as a crime when there is no law punishing it.

What is Crime?

Crime is an act or omission punishable by public laws.

Four specific terms of crime

  1. Felonies — violations of the Revised Penal Code.
  2. Offenses — violations of statutes or Special Laws.
  3. Infraction or Misdemeanor — violations of ordinances.
  4. Delinquency — violations committed by minor offenders.

Concepts of crime, law and justice

The 1987 Philippine Constitution provided the distribution of the great powers of government to three branches: executive, legislative and judiciary. These branches are co-equal and observe separation of powers.

Limitations on the legislative branch in enacting penal legislation

  1. No ex post facto law or bill of attainder shall be enacted.
  2. Ex post facto law makes an act done before the passage of the law punishable.
  3. Bill of attainder is a legislative act which inflicts punishment without trial.
  4. No person shall be held to answer for a criminal offense without due process of law.
2. Criminal and Victim

Criminal

Any person who commits a crime is basically a criminal. In the criminal justice system, different terms are used in every stage or level of the justice system.

Criminological sense

A person who committed a crime regardless of whether it was reported or referred.

Legal sense

A person who was found guilty beyond reasonable doubt.

Terms used at different stages

  1. Person of Interest — when there are several suspicious persons.
  2. Suspect — when there is one suspected person left.
  3. Respondent — when the matter is within the level of prosecution.
  4. Accused — when the case is already pending before the court.
  5. Persons Deprived of Liberty — persons under correctional facilities.
  6. Ex-convict or Balik-bayan — when they are back in the community.

Victim

Victim is the scientific study of the extent, nature and causes of criminal victimization, its consequences for the persons involved and the reactions of society, particularly the police and the criminal justice system, as well as voluntary workers and professional helpers.

Types of Victim

Type Description Example
Primary Victim The person who experiences the crime first-hand. A person directly victimized by a crime.
Secondary Victim Individuals who experience or are related to the primary victim. Parent, cousin, sibling or relative.
Tertiary Victim Individuals who watch or read the story and are affected by it. People affected by hearing or reading about the crime.

Victimless Crimes

These are crimes against the law but harm the individual who consented to engage in them. There is no private offended party in these crimes.

  • Gambling
  • Prostitution
  • Possession of firearms
  • Drug-related cases
3. Evolution of Law

Code of Hammurabi

  • One of the first known bodies of law.
  • Promulgated by King Hammurabi of ancient Babylon.
  • Engraved on stone tablets and dealt with theft, ownership, sexual relationships and violence.

Roman Law

Derived from the Twelve Tables, a collection of basic rules related to family, religion and economic life.

Common Law

  • Its origin is traced from early English society.
  • A body of unwritten legal principles created from social customs, rules and practices.

The Magna Carta

  • Signed on June 15, 1215 by King John of England.
  • It guarantees basic liberties for British citizens.

Greek Laws

Contributed to Western thoughts about law through ideas of philosophers such as Socrates, Plato and Aristotle.

Canon Law

  • Also known as Ecclesiastical Law.
  • Adopted for the government of the Christian organization and its members.

Germanic Law

A family of customary laws from the Germanic tribes.

Philippine Laws

  • For early Filipinos, laws were both written and unwritten.
  • Written laws were decreed and codified, while unwritten laws were based on customs and traditions.

Maragtas Code

  • A written code in the pre-Spanish era.
  • Described in the handout as the oldest body of laws in the Philippines.
  • Written by Datu Sumakwel of Panay in 1250 A.D.

Kalantiaw Code

  • Described in the handout as the second oldest legal code in the Philippines.
  • Written by Datu Kalantiaw of Panay in 1433 A.D.
4. Justice and the Criminal Justice System

Definition of Justice

Justice is the act of rendering what are due and treating persons equally. These persons must, however, fall within the same classification.

Justice under the 1987 Philippine Constitution

Under Section 1, Article III of the 1987 Philippine Constitution, no person shall be deprived of life, liberty or property without due process of law, nor shall any person be denied equal protection of the laws.

Essence / Types of Justice

  1. Procedural Justice — focuses on the fairness of the processes by which decisions are made. Fair, unbiased and respectful legal processes enhance public trust and compliance with the law.
  2. Distributive Justice — pertains to the fair allocation of resources and penalties; the distribution of burdens and benefits should be equitable and based on principles of fairness and equality.
  3. Restorative Justice — emphasizes repairing the harm caused by criminal behavior and fostering reconciliation between offenders and victims. It involves stakeholders such as victims, offenders and the community in dialogue to address harm, seek accountability and restore relationships.

Kinds of Due Process

  • Procedural due process — ensures procedures and methods used by government before depriving a person of life, liberty or property are fair and just. Essential elements include notice, hearing and judgment.
  • Substantive due process — protects individuals from unreasonable, oppressive or arbitrary laws, regulations and policies.

Criminal Justice System

The criminal justice system is the machinery used by the state to enforce rules of conduct necessary for the protection of life and property and to maintain peace and order.

As a social institution: CJS attempts to meet society's needs for law and order.

As a system: CJS depends on due process involving the five pillars.

Five Pillars

Law Enforcement → Prosecution → Court → Correction → Community

Comparative Criminal Justice

This looks into different models of countries to investigate and evaluate a rational system of justice in terms of criminal justice process, culture and institutions.

SECOND PRELIM — Concepts and Philosophy of Prosecution

1. Prosecution

Meaning of Prosecution

Prosecution is the institution or process of bringing a criminal action before the court for the determination of the guilt or innocence of the accused.

Concept of Prosecution

The prosecution is an important part of the criminal justice system. It works with law enforcement agencies in evaluating criminal complaints, examining evidence and determining whether a criminal action should be filed.

Purpose of Prosecution

  • To determine whether there is sufficient basis to prosecute a person.
  • To represent the State in criminal proceedings.
  • To present evidence before the court.
  • To protect the interests of justice.
2. Prosecutor

Role of the Prosecutor

  • Receives and evaluates criminal complaints.
  • Examines the evidence submitted in support of a complaint.
  • Conducts proceedings required by law.
  • Determines whether the evidence supports the filing of a criminal action.
  • Represents the State in criminal proceedings before the court.
  • Presents evidence and arguments during the prosecution of a case.

Prosecution and Law Enforcement

Law enforcement officers investigate reported offenses, gather information, preserve evidence and identify persons who may have committed an offense. The prosecutor performs the legal evaluation required for prosecution.

Cooperation between investigators and prosecutors is important because criminal cases require evidence that is legally sufficient and properly presented.

3. Preliminary Investigation

Meaning

Preliminary investigation is a proceeding to determine whether there is sufficient ground to engender a well-founded belief that a crime has been committed and that the respondent is probably guilty thereof and should be held for trial.

Purpose of Preliminary Investigation

  1. To determine whether a criminal case should be filed in court.
  2. To protect persons from the inconvenience and expense of a trial when the evidence does not support prosecution.
  3. To determine whether the evidence gives sufficient basis for holding the respondent for trial.
  4. To allow the parties to present their respective sides according to the applicable procedure.
4. Investigation and Prosecution

Investigation

Primarily concerns the gathering, preservation and development of information and evidence relating to an alleged offense.

Prosecution

Concerns the legal evaluation and presentation of a criminal case in behalf of the State.

MIDTERM — The Court Pillar

1. The Court

What is a Court?

A court is a body to which the public administration of justice is delegated. It is a tribunal or forum established by law and authorized to hear and determine cases at the appropriate time and place.

In criminal cases, the court determines the issues presented before it according to the Constitution, laws, rules and evidence properly submitted in the proceedings.

2. Historical Development of the Judiciary

Judicial System of the Pre-Spanish Filipinos

Before Spanish colonization, there were no written laws in the modern sense. Rules were derived from customs, usages and traditions. Judicial authority was exercised by the datu, who acted as a judge in settling disputes.

Judicial System under the Spanish Regime

During the Spanish period, the judicial system was organized under Spanish colonial authority. King Philip II of Spain established the Real Audiencia de Manila, which performed judicial as well as executive and administrative functions.

Judicial System under the American Occupation

The judicial system was reorganized during the American period. Existing laws and courts were recognized and new institutions were established as part of the development of the Philippine judicial system.

Establishment of the Supreme Court of the Philippines

Act No. 136, enacted in 1901, provided for the organization of the courts in the Philippine Islands and formally established the Supreme Court of the Philippines.

Supreme Court under the 1973 Constitution

The 1973 Constitution changed several aspects of the governmental system, including the organization of the judiciary. The membership of the Supreme Court was increased to fifteen: one Chief Justice and fourteen Associate Justices.

Supreme Court under the Revolutionary Government

On March 25, 1986, President Corazon Aquino abolished the 1973 Constitution through Proclamation No. 3 and put in place a provisional constitutional arrangement. Provisions concerning the judiciary were adopted during this transitional period.

Supreme Court under the 1987 Constitution

The 1987 Constitution continues to vest judicial power in the Supreme Court and such lower courts as may be established by law. Judicial power includes the duty of the courts to settle actual controversies involving rights that are legally demandable and enforceable.

3. Judicial Power

Meaning of Judicial Power

Judicial power includes the duty of the courts of justice to settle actual controversies involving rights which are legally demandable and enforceable.

It also includes the determination of whether or not there has been a grave abuse of discretion amounting to lack or excess of jurisdiction on the part of any branch or instrumentality of the Government.

Power of Judicial Review

Judicial review is the power of the Supreme Court to determine whether laws passed by Congress and acts of the President are in accordance with the Constitution when the constitutional issue is properly raised before the Court.

4. Types of Courts

Supreme Court

The Supreme Court is the highest court in the Philippines. It is composed of the Chief Justice and fourteen Associate Justices. It is the final arbiter of judicial issues and may sit en banc or in divisions as provided by the Constitution and law.

Court of Appeals

The Court of Appeals is an appellate court that reviews decisions brought before it from the lower courts and other bodies as provided by law. It is composed of a Presiding Justice and Associate Justices.

Regional Trial Court

Regional Trial Courts exercise the jurisdiction granted to them by law, including original and appellate jurisdiction over specified civil and criminal cases.

Metropolitan Trial Court

Metropolitan Trial Courts exercise the jurisdiction assigned by law in metropolitan areas.

Municipal Trial Court

Municipal Trial Courts exercise the jurisdiction assigned by law in municipalities and other areas covered by their respective courts.

Municipal Circuit Trial Court

Municipal Circuit Trial Courts serve areas composed of municipalities grouped together pursuant to law and exercise the jurisdiction provided by law.

5. Jurisdiction and Venue

Jurisdiction

Jurisdiction refers to the power or authority given by law to a court or tribunal to hear and decide a case and to enforce its judgment.

Venue

Venue refers to the particular geographical area in which a court with jurisdiction may hear or determine a case.

Groups of Jurisdiction

  1. Criminal Jurisdiction — power and authority to hear cases involving criminal acts.
  2. Civil Jurisdiction — power and authority to hear cases which are civil in nature.
